The GPU's block scheduler places blocks on SMs until every SM is full, then waits for blocks to finish before placing more. If every
block takes about the same time, which is true for a GEMM where every block computes one output tile, the grid runs in **waves**:

$$
\text{waves} = \left\lceil \frac{\text{blocks}}{\text{SMs} \times \text{blocks per SM}} \right\rceil
$$

A wave takes as long as one block, however many blocks it contains. So the run time is a **staircase** in the number of blocks, and a grid
whose last wave is nearly empty pays a full wave's time for a sliver of work. This is **wave quantization**.

## Measured: the staircase

A synthetic kernel where every block spins for the same number of cycles, and only one block fits per SM:

```text title="output (RTX 3050 6GB Laptop, 20 SMs)"
Part 1: every block spins for 1000000 cycles; 1 block(s) fit per SM, 20 SMs:
   blocks   waves      time      SM busy
        1       1   0.67 ms           5%
       10       1   0.68 ms          50%
       19       1   0.68 ms          95%
       20       1   0.68 ms         100%
       21       2   1.35 ms          52%
       30       2   1.35 ms          75%
       39       2   1.35 ms          98%
       40       2   1.35 ms         100%
       41       3   2.02 ms          68%
       60       3   2.02 ms         100%
       61       4   2.69 ms          76%
```

One block takes as long as twenty. Twenty-one blocks take **twice** as long as twenty: one extra block adds a full wave.

## In a real GEMM

The lab repeats this with the register-tiled matmul from [6.2](/learn/m06/register-tiling): $M = 1280$, $K = 4096$, and $N$ growing one
128-wide tile at a time. Two blocks fit per SM, so a wave is 40 tiles:

```text title="output"
Part 2: 1280 x N x 4096 matmul, 128 x 128 tiles; 2 blocks fit per SM, so 40 tiles per wave:
       N  tiles  waves      time    GFLOP/s  ms per tile
     128     10      1   0.75 ms       1796        0.075
     256     20      1   0.75 ms       3573        0.038
     384     30      1   1.00 ms       4012        0.033
     512     40      1   1.02 ms       5264        0.025
     640     50      2   1.82 ms       3696        0.036
     768     60      2   2.01 ms       4010        0.033
     896     70      2   2.34 ms       4012        0.033
    1024     80      2   2.35 ms       4569        0.029
    1152     90      3   3.19 ms       3790        0.035
```

Going from 40 to 50 tiles is **25% more work and 78% more time**. Throughput peaks exactly at full waves (40 and 80 tiles) and dips just
after them.

The steps are uneven within a wave, because two blocks share each SM. With 10 or 20 tiles, every block has an SM to itself and runs
faster; at 30 or 40, pairs of blocks share SMs and each runs slower. The quantum that matters is really "how evenly is work spread across
SMs", and full waves are the even case.

## What to do about it

- **Pick shapes that fit.** Model dimensions are chosen as multiples of 64 or 128 partly for this reason. A vocabulary of 50,257 tokens
  (GPT-2) is often padded to 50,304, a multiple of 64, for faster LM-head GEMMs.
- **Let the library choose tiles.** cuBLAS and CUTLASS heuristics pick a tile size per shape, partly to make the tile count land near a
  multiple of the SM count. That is one reason they beat a fixed-tile kernel on odd shapes.
- **Split-K.** For skinny GEMMs with few output tiles but a long $K$, such as decode at small batch sizes, split the $K$ loop across
  several blocks and add the partial results at the end (with atomics, or deterministically as in [5.3](/learn/m05/atomics-and-cas)). More
  blocks means fuller waves.
- **Stream-K.** Give each SM an equal share of the total *k-iterations*, crossing tile boundaries, so every SM finishes at the same
  time. CUTLASS implements it, and it removes the tail entirely at the cost of fix-up work.
- **Persistent kernels.** Launch exactly one block per SM slot and let each block loop over tiles from a queue. The same idea underlies
  persistent attention and MoE kernels.

<Callout type="insight" title="Why this matters more on big GPUs">
  With 132 SMs, an H100 needs 132 tiles (or a multiple) for one even wave. During decode with a small batch, a GEMM's output often has fewer
  tiles than that, so most of the GPU idles. That is the case split-K and FlashDecoding ([9.5](/learn/m09/split-kv-flash-decoding)) were
  designed for.
</Callout>
