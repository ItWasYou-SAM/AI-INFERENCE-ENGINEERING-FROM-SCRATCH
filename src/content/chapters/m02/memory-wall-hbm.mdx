Arithmetic is easy to add to a chip: more lanes, wider tensor cores, lower precision. Bandwidth is hard to add, because every byte has to
cross the edge of the chip through physical wires. Over time, the two have drifted apart.

## The gap, in numbers

| GPU | Year | Dense FP16/BF16 tensor | Memory bandwidth | FLOP per byte (ridge) |
|:--|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| V100 SXM2 | 2017 | 125 TFLOP/s | 0.90 TB/s | 139 |
| A100 SXM 80GB | 2020 | 312 TFLOP/s | 2.04 TB/s | 153 |
| H100 SXM | 2022 | 989 TFLOP/s | 3.35 TB/s | 295 |
| H200 SXM | 2024 | 989 TFLOP/s | 4.8 TB/s | 206 |
| B200 | 2024 | ~2,250 TFLOP/s | ~8 TB/s | ~281 |

From V100 to B200, tensor throughput grew about **18×** while bandwidth grew about **9×**. The ridge point, the arithmetic intensity a
kernel needs before compute becomes the limit ([3.3](/learn/m03/roofline)), has roughly doubled. A kernel that was balanced on a V100 is
memory-bound on a B200. This growing imbalance is called the **memory wall**.

<Callout type="insight" title="What this means for decode">
  Decode at small batch has an arithmetic intensity of about 1–10 FLOP per byte, far below every ridge point in the table. For decode, the
  column that sets speed is **bandwidth**. The H200 has the same compute as the H100 but 43% more bandwidth, and it decodes about that much
  faster. Compute upgrades alone do little for decode.
</Callout>

## How HBM gets its bandwidth

Bandwidth is roughly **bus width × transfer rate per wire**. Ordinary DRAM and GDDR sit on a circuit board, where each wire is long, so the
bus is narrow (64 bits per DDR channel, 384 bits on an RTX 4090) and the wires have to run very fast.

**High Bandwidth Memory** goes wide instead:

- Several DRAM dies are **stacked** vertically and connected by thousands of through-silicon vias (TSVs).
- Each stack has a **1,024-bit** interface, 16 times wider than a DDR channel.
- The stacks sit on a silicon **interposer** right beside the GPU die, millimetres away, so all those wires are short and dense.

An H100 SXM uses five active HBM3 stacks: a 5,120-bit bus delivering 3.35 TB/s from 80 GB. An RTX 4090's GDDR6X reaches about 1 TB/s from
24 GB with a 384-bit bus running much faster per pin.

| | GDDR6X (RTX 4090) | HBM3 (H100 SXM) |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Placement | separate chips on the board | stacked beside the die, on an interposer |
| Bus width | 384 bits | 5,120 bits |
| Bandwidth | ~1.0 TB/s | 3.35 TB/s |
| Capacity | 24 GB | 80 GB |
| Cost and supply | cheap, plentiful | expensive, supply-limited packaging |

<Callout type="warning" title="The cost of the wall">
  HBM and its advanced packaging are a large part of an accelerator's cost, and their supply has limited how many GPUs could be built.
  Capacity is a wall too: an 80 GB GPU cannot hold a 70B model in BF16 (140 GB) at all. Every byte of HBM is fought over by weights and
  KV cache ([9.1](/learn/m09/kv-cache-size)).
</Callout>

## The speed limit, as a formula

If a decode step has to read the weights $W$ bytes and the batch's KV cache $K$ bytes from HBM with bandwidth $B$, then no kernel can make the
step faster than

$$
t_{\text{step}} \;\geq\; \frac{W + K}{B}.
$$

For Llama-3-8B in BF16 on an H100 at short context: $16\ \text{GB} / 3.35\ \text{TB/s} \approx 4.8\ \text{ms}$, so at most about
**210 tokens/s** for a single stream, however clever the kernels are. The ways around it are all about bytes: read fewer
([quantization](/learn/m12/number-formats)), share each read across more requests ([batching](/learn/m10/continuous-batching)), or
spread the bytes over more GPUs' worth of bandwidth ([tensor parallelism](/learn/m14/tensor-parallelism)).
